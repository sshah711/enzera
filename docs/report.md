## Abstract

Electricity theft causes large non-technical losses for utilities, but inspectors can visit only a few customers. They need a
ranked list of suspicious customers with a checkable reason for each. ENZERA scores customers for theft risk from their daily
smart-meter readings, explains every score with exact TreeSHAP contributions, estimates a calibrated theft probability, adds an
unsupervised abnormal-consumption score, and supports the whole inspection workflow in a deployed web application. The workflow
covers planning, cases with roles and an audit trail, retraining from confirmed outcomes, and drift monitoring.

On the SGCC dataset (42,372 customers, 1,034 days, 8.53% theft), our XGBoost model on 17 engineered features reaches ROC-AUC 0.798
and PR-AUC 0.376 on a held-out test set. If inspectors visit the 10% highest-scored customers, they find **43.5%** of all thieves
(random: 10%). Computing features only on the days each meter actually reported, plus two weekly-pattern features, improved PR-AUC
from 0.332 to 0.376 (bootstrap 95% CI of the gain [+0.017, +0.067]). SMOTE and hyperparameter changes did not help. A 1D-CNN on the
raw daily series is not better on its own, but an XGBoost + CNN ensemble reaches PR-AUC 0.433. It is not deployed, to keep exact
explanations. A synthetic-attack test shows an important limitation: the model learned what *caught* SGCC thieves look like, and
does not detect textbook manipulation patterns injected into honest customers.

## 1. Introduction and problem

**Background.** Distribution losses are *technical* (energy lost in lines and transformers) or *non-technical*, mainly theft. Smart
meters record each customer's consumption every day, which makes it possible to detect theft from consumption patterns.

**Problem.** Given a customer's daily consumption history, estimate how likely the customer is to be stealing electricity, so that a
limited number of inspections can be focused on the most suspicious customers, and explain why.

**Why it is hard.**
1. **Class imbalance**: 8.53% thieves.
2. **Messy data**: 25.7% of readings missing in the training split, often in long blocks; 22.3% of customers have no reading before
   2016.
3. **Overlap**: no single feature reaches ROC-AUC 0.71 on its own (Section 3).
4. **Trust**: inspectors need reasons, not bare scores.
5. **Label bias**: labels come from past inspections, i.e. from who was *caught*.

**Objectives.** A supervised risk model compared against baselines with imbalance-aware metrics; an unsupervised abnormality
detector; exact per-customer explanations; calibrated probabilities; a working product (API, database, web app, roles, audit
trail, retraining, monitoring, deployment, CI); and honest evaluation (threshold and calibration fitted on validation, metrics on
a held-out test set, an app that shows only never-seen customers, and model changes adopted only under decision rules fixed in
advance).

## 2. Literature review

**Zheng et al. (2018), Wide & Deep CNN on SGCC.** Zheng, Yang, Niu, Dai and Zhou released the SGCC dataset used here (daily
consumption of 42,372 customers of the State Grid Corporation of China, January 2014 - October 2016, with theft labels). Their model
has two parts. The *Wide* part is a fully connected network on the raw 1-D daily series, which learns global patterns. The *Deep*
part is a CNN on the consumption reshaped into a 2-D weeks × days matrix, which captures weekly periodicity and how it is disrupted.
They interpolate missing values and cap outliers before modelling, and evaluate with ROC-AUC and MAP@N (precision among the top-N
ranked customers), a ranking view similar to our "theft caught in the top 10%".
*Relation to our work:* we use their dataset and test their two key ideas. (1) We added weekly-periodicity features
(`weekly_autocorr`, `dow_strength`). (2) We trained a 1D-CNN on the raw daily series (notebook 05). Our main model stays a
gradient-boosted tree on interpretable features, because it gives exact explanations and runs on free hosting. Results are not
directly comparable to theirs (different splits and protocol).

**Jokar et al. (2016), theft attack types.** Jokar, Arianpoo and Leung studied theft detection in AMI using customers' consumption
patterns. They formalised *false-data injection attacks*, i.e. ways a thief manipulates reported consumption:
- reducing readings by a constant or random factor
- reporting zero during some periods
- reporting a constant (e.g. average) value instead of the real profile
- re-ordering readings so the total stays the same but the cost under time-of-use pricing drops

They used such synthetic attacks to train and test a support vector machine on each customer's pattern.
*Relation to our work:* several of our features target these attack families (drop, trend, recent-vs-past and low-day share for
reductions; zero share and longest zero run for zero periods; variability features for constant values). In notebook 04 we
**inject daily versions of these attacks into honest test customers** to see whether our model notices (Section 6.5).
Re-ordering within a day cannot be studied with daily data.

**References**

1. Z. Zheng, Y. Yang, X. Niu, H.-N. Dai and Y. Zhou, "Wide and Deep Convolutional Neural Networks for Electricity-Theft Detection
   to Secure Smart Grids," *IEEE Transactions on Industrial Informatics*, vol. 14, no. 4, pp. 1606-1615, 2018,
   doi: 10.1109/TII.2017.2785963.
2. P. Jokar, N. Arianpoo and V. C. M. Leung, "Electricity Theft Detection in AMI Using Customers' Consumption Patterns,"
   *IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid*, vol. 7, no. 1, pp. 216-226, 2016, doi: 10.1109/TSG.2015.2425222.

| Property | Value |
|---|---|

| Split (stratified, seed 42) | 29,660 train (70%) / 6,356 test (15%) / 6,356 demo (15%) |
| Train used for fitting / validation | 25,211 / 4,449 |

*Train* fits the models (15% held out as *validation* for the threshold and calibration). *Test* is used for the reported metrics.
*Demo* customers are never used in training or evaluation. 1,000 of them are loaded into the app, so the demo shows genuine
out-of-sample predictions. All EDA uses the **train split only** (`notebooks/01_eda.ipynb`).

**3.1 Imbalance.** 27,130 normal vs 2,530 theft (1 : 10.7). Always predicting "normal" would give 91.5% accuracy and catch nobody.
[FIGURE: reports/eda_class_balance.png]

**3.2 Missing data.** 25.7% of readings are missing, and every customer has at least one missing day. A large group misses about
71% of days: **22.3% of customers have no reading before 2016** (730 of 1,034 days). About 35% of customers are missing on a typical
2014-2015 day, and 1.6% in 2016. Thieves miss more (median missing ratio 21.4% vs 9.7%).
[FIGURE: reports/eda_missing.png]

**3.3 Zero usage** is rare in both classes (median zero-day share 0.7% normal, 0.3% theft).

therefore labels, focus on larger and more irregular customers.
[FIGURE: reports/eda_avg_consumption.png]

**3.5 Single-feature separation** (ROC-AUC on train): `std_daily` 0.705, `max_daily` 0.693, `mean_daily` 0.654, `recent_vs_past`

**3.6 Sample curves** show flat interpolated stretches for meters without pre-2016 data, a thief whose usage collapses for almost

## 4. Preprocessing and the 17 features

All preprocessing is in `ml/features.py` and is shared by training **and** the backend, so there is no train/serve skew.

**Gap-aware cleaning (`clean_inside`).**
1. Gaps *between* a customer's readings are linearly interpolated. Days before the first reading or after the last one stay
   missing; they are not filled.
2. Negative readings are clipped to 0.
3. Outliers are capped per customer at mean + 3 standard deviations.
4. Every feature uses NaN-aware statistics on the days that exist.

**Why "gap-aware"?** The original pipeline interpolated in both directions, so for the 22.3% of meters that started in 2016 the
whole of 2014-2015 became a flat copy of their first reading. That distorted variability and trend. Notebook 03 shows the gap-aware
version is clearly better (Section 6.3).

**Input formats.** The API accepts SGCC wide format or long format (`customer_id, date, consumption`). Customers with all readings
missing are kept; `pivot_table` used to drop them silently, a bug we fixed and covered with a regression test.

**Features.** `x` = the cleaned daily series on observed days. Monthly means use observed days only. "Recent" = the last 90 days.

| # | Feature | Meaning shown to inspectors | How it is computed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `mean_daily` | Average daily consumption (kWh) | mean of `x` |
| 2 | `std_daily` | Day-to-day variation (kWh) | standard deviation of `x` |
| 3 | `cv_daily` | Relative variability of consumption | std / mean |
| 4 | `max_daily` | Highest daily consumption (kWh) | max of `x` |
| 5 | `missing_ratio` | Share of days with missing readings | share of raw readings that are missing (over all 1,034 days) |
| 6 | `zero_ratio` | Share of days with zero consumption | share of observed days with `x` ≤ 1e-6 |
| 7 | `longest_zero_run` | Longest run of zero-consumption days | longest run of observed zero days |
| 8 | `low_day_ratio` | Share of days far below the usual level | share of observed days with `x` < 0.2 × mean |
| 9 | `spike_ratio` | Share of days with unusual spikes | share of observed days with `x` > mean + 2 std |
| 10 | `trend_slope` | Long-term trend of monthly usage | least-squares slope of monthly means (observed months), divided by the mean |
| 11 | `max_monthly_drop` | Largest month-to-month drop | max of (m[t-1] - m[t]) / m[t-1], clipped to [0, 1] |
| 12 | `monthly_cv` | Variability between months | std / mean of monthly means |
| 13 | `recent_vs_past` | Recent usage compared with earlier usage | mean of last 90 days / mean of earlier days, clipped to [0, 10] |
| 14 | `weekend_ratio` | Weekend vs weekday usage | weekend mean / weekday mean, clipped to [0, 10] |
| 15 | `active_span_ratio` | Share of the period the meter was reporting | (last reading day - first reading day + 1) / 1,034 |
| 16 | `weekly_autocorr` | Weekly repetition of the usage pattern | correlation between usage and usage 7 days earlier |
| 17 | `dow_strength` | Strength of the day-of-week cycle | variance of the 7 weekday means / variance of daily usage |

The features fall into these groups: **level** (1, 4), **variability** (2, 3, 9, 12), **suspicious low usage** (6, 7, 8, 11), **change
over time** (10, 13), **weekly pattern** (14, 16, 17) and **data quality** (5, 15). The middle column is exactly what the app shows.

## 5. Models

| Model | Role | Settings | Imbalance handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logistic Regression | linear baseline | StandardScaler + LR | `class_weight="balanced"` |
| Random Forest | non-linear baseline | 300 trees, min_samples_leaf 2 | `class_weight="balanced_subsample"` |
| **XGBoost** | **main model** | 400 trees, depth 5, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8, colsample 0.8 | `scale_pos_weight` = 10.721 (#normal / #theft) |
| Isolation Forest | abnormal consumption | 300 trees, fitted on **normal** training customers only | unsupervised |
| 1D-CNN (research) | deep baseline on raw series | 3 conv blocks (16/32/64 filters), 18,545 parameters | `pos_weight` = 10.7 in the loss |

**Why XGBoost?** It is the standard strong choice for tabular data. It captures non-linear effects and interactions (each feature is
weak alone), needs no scaling, trains in seconds on a CPU, and supports **exact TreeSHAP** via `pred_contribs=True`.

**Threshold.** Chosen to maximise F1 on the **validation** split: **0.656**. Bands: High ≥ 0.656, Medium ≥ 0.328, otherwise Low.

**Calibration.** Class weights inflate raw scores, so they are not probabilities. A Platt-scaling layer (a logistic regression on
the logit of the score, fitted on validation) converts them. Test Brier score: raw 0.122 → **0.065**. Isotonic regression was
almost as good (0.066), but its top step claimed 100% based on 4 validation customers, so we chose the smooth Platt curve.

**Metrics.** Precision, recall, F1 (at the threshold); ROC-AUC; **PR-AUC** (random = 0.085); **theft caught in the top 10%**; and the
full **capture curve** (share of thieves found for every inspection budget from 1% to 100%), which drives the Inspection planner.

### 6.3 Feature experiments: gap handling and weekly patterns (`notebooks/03_feature_experiments.ipynb`)

**Rule (fixed before running, same for all experiments):** adopt a change only if it improves test PR-AUC **and** top-10% recall by
≥ 0.01 each, without being worse on validation PR-AUC.

| Variant | Precision | Recall | F1 | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Top 10% | PR-AUC (val) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. original 14 features | 0.363 | 0.366 | 0.365 | 0.788 | 0.332 | 0.405 | 0.338 |
| B. gap-aware (15) | 0.425 | 0.363 | 0.391 | 0.795 | 0.366 | 0.424 | 0.374 |
| C. weekly only (16) | 0.391 | 0.319 | 0.351 | 0.788 | 0.329 | 0.398 | 0.334 |
| **D. gap-aware + weekly (17)** | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.798 | **0.376** | **0.435** | **0.379** |

D was adopted. The PR-AUC gain is +0.043, with a paired bootstrap 95% CI of [+0.017, +0.067], so it is not noise. Weekly features
*alone* (C) hurt, because with the old cleaning they were computed on artificial flat segments. They only help once gaps are left
unfilled. This is the model deployed now.

### 6.4 Imbalance handling and hyperparameters (`notebooks/02_experiments.ipynb`, run on the original 14-feature model)

| Setting | PR-AUC | Top 10% | PR-AUC (val) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Class weights (used) | 0.332 | 0.405 | 0.338 |
| SMOTE | 0.296 | 0.366 | 0.271 |
| Depth 3, 800 trees | 0.322 | 0.394 | 0.338 |
| Depth 7, regularised | 0.328 | 0.394 | 0.331 |
| Depth 6, 1,500 trees, lr 0.02 | 0.332 | 0.405 | 0.336 |

None qualified. SMOTE is worse on everything: because the classes overlap, synthetic thieves land among normal customers. The best
alternative ties the used model (bootstrap CI of the difference [-0.015, +0.014]). Conclusion: features matter more than tuning,
which is what led to the experiments in 6.3. [FIGURE: reports/experiments.png]

### 6.5 Synthetic theft attacks (`notebooks/04_synthetic_attacks.ipynb`)

We applied daily versions of Jokar et al.'s attacks to 4,471 **honest** test customers (each attack starting 6-24 months before the
end of the data) and re-scored them with the deployed model.

| Attack | In top-10% risk zone after attack | Median risk before → after |
|---|---|---|
| (none: same customers) | 7.0% | 0.174 |
| A1 constant reduction (× 0.2-0.8) | 3.6% | 0.174 → 0.181 |
| A2 zero period (30-180 days) | 8.1% | 0.174 → 0.228 |
| A3 random daily reduction | 3.4% | 0.174 → 0.186 |
| A4 noisy average | 2.2% | 0.174 → 0.165 |
| A5 flat average | 2.9% | 0.174 → 0.082 |
| A6 reversed order | 7.4% | 0.174 → 0.181 |

**The model does not detect these attacks.** Only zero periods raise scores, and only slightly. Reducing or flattening usage makes
customers look *safer*. The model learned the profile of *caught* SGCC thieves (larger, variable, rising usage), not the mechanics
of manipulation. We see this as an important, honest limitation: ENZERA finds customers who resemble past confirmed thieves, and
should be complemented by self-comparison rules and retrained as new inspection outcomes arrive.
[FIGURE: reports/attack_detection.png]

### 6.6 Deep learning: 1D-CNN on raw daily data (`notebooks/05_cnn_baseline.ipynb`)

Input: 2 channels × 1,034 days (log usage after gap-aware cleaning, plus an observed/missing mask). Training used early stopping on
validation PR-AUC; the best epoch was 12 of 15.

| Model | Precision | Recall | F1 | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Top 10% | PR-AUC (val) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| XGBoost, 17 features (deployed) | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.798 | 0.376 | 0.435 | 0.379 |
| 1D-CNN, raw series | 0.342 | 0.512 | 0.410 | 0.811 | 0.391 | 0.459 | 0.346 |
| **Ensemble (rank average)** | **0.414** | 0.440 | **0.427** | **0.831** | **0.433** | **0.466** | **0.404** |

- **The CNN alone fails the rule.** It is better on test but worse on validation.
- **The ensemble passes it clearly:** PR-AUC +0.058, bootstrap CI [+0.034, +0.080]. The two models agree only moderately
  (Spearman 0.541), so they catch different thieves.
- **The ensemble is not deployed.** Exact TreeSHAP explanations exist only for the tree half, and PyTorch does not fit the free 512 MB
  host. A deployment path exists (export the CNN to ONNX, run it with `onnxruntime`); this is future work.

### 6.7 Retraining from inspection outcomes (`ml/retrain.py`, `reports/retrain_report.json`)

**Simulation:** inspectors visit all 80 High-band demo customers and record their true outcome (34 theft, 46 normal). The script
adds these labels to the training data (never touching the test split), retrains, and compares the result with the current model
on the fixed test set.

| | Precision | Recall | F1 | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Top 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.394 | 0.798 | 0.376 | 0.435 |
| Candidate (+80 labels) | 0.422 | 0.363 | 0.390 | 0.798 | 0.371 | 0.418 |

The candidate is slightly worse, so the **promotion gate refuses to deploy it**. That is the correct outcome: 80 labels are 0.3% more
data, and they agree with the existing SGCC labels, so they carry no new information. Retraining pays off when outcomes *disagree*
with the old labels or cover new theft styles.

## 7. Explainability

(Full write-up with three case studies: `docs/explainability.md`.)

- **Reasons.** TreeSHAP splits each score exactly: `risk = sigmoid(0.173 + Σ SHAP)`. The app shows the top 3 positive contributions,
  a **waterfall** of all 17 contributions, and a **what-if** simulator. For example, lowering the top customer's day-to-day variation
  from 13.86 to 1.96 kWh drops its risk from 0.97 to 0.90 and its estimated probability from 80% to 55%.
- **Case studies.**
  - `448FE010…`: risk 0.971 (actually theft). A 65% drop in July 2014, nine months at 3-8 kWh/day, then recovery to 20-57 kWh/day.
  - `6FDB63D7…`: risk 0.970 (actually theft). The same drop-and-recover pattern, starting in April 2014.
  - `F21D74D3…`: risk 0.0025 (normal). Reads almost exactly zero for three years. The supervised model is confident it is not a
    thief, but the **Isolation Forest scores it 0.90**: one example of how the two detectors complement each other.
- **Global importance** (mean |SHAP|, test): day-to-day variation 0.363, missing readings 0.330, trend 0.306, peak usage 0.286,
  between-month variability 0.200, active-reporting share 0.199 (new) ... low-day share 0.080. Importance is correlational, not causal.
  [FIGURE: reports/shap_importance.png]
- **Isolation Forest:** weak as a theft ranker (ROC-AUC 0.586, top-10% 13.8%), uncorrelated with risk (Spearman −0.039), but useful
  for label-free anomalies.
- **Reliability on unseen demo customers:** 34 of the 80 High-band customers are thieves (42.5%, about 5× the base rate); 26 of the
  85 demo thieves are scored Low.

```
                │ → calibration, capture curve, drift reference → model bundle      │
                │ ml/retrain.py: + confirmed inspection labels → gated promotion    │
                └─────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┘
                                                  │ git push
     GitHub ──► Actions CI (20 tests + Docker build)
       │
       ├──► Render (Docker, free): FastAPI backend ◄──── HTTPS / JSON ────► Streamlit Cloud: frontend (6 pages)
       │      · scores 1,000 demo customers at startup                       · dashboard · customer detail (reasons,
       │      · SHAP, what-if, similar customers, drift (PSI)                  waterfall, what-if, similar, PDF sheet)

       ▼
  https://enzera-api.onrender.com                                          https://enzera.streamlit.app
```

**Communication.** The frontend never loads the model. It calls the REST API with `requests` (JSON over HTTPS) at the URL in the
`BACKEND_URL` secret, sending an `Authorization: Bearer <token>` header after login and an optional `X-API-Key`.

**Roles.** Viewing is public. An **inspector** can open cases and add notes. A **manager** can also record outcomes (theft confirmed /
normal), reopen or delete cases, and export training labels. Tokens are HMAC-signed and expire after 12 hours. Every action on a case
is written to `case_events` (who, role, when, status before → after, note), and history is kept even after deletion.

**API endpoints** (`backend/main.py`, interactive docs at `/docs`)

| Method | Path | Access | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| GET | `/health` | public | status, model version, customers scored, database type |
| POST | `/auth/login` · GET `/auth/me` | public · logged in | get a token · who am I |
| GET | `/stats` | public | customers per band, cases per status, threshold |
| GET | `/customers` | public | customers ranked by risk (filter by band), with reasons and probability |

**Monitoring.** For each feature, the backend compares new data with the training deciles using the **Population Stability Index**
(< 0.10 stable, 0.10-0.25 moderate, > 0.25 major). The demo customers are stable (largest PSI 0.021). The one-year sample upload file
shows a major shift on 14 of 17 features, a correct warning that scores on short histories are less reliable.

**Quality.** 20 automated tests cover:
- date sorting, cleaning, gap-aware and weekly features
- input formats and the all-missing regression
- API ranking, prediction and bad input
- SHAP additivity and what-if behaviour
- similar customers and drift
- login, roles and the full case lifecycle with audit trail
- label export and the PDF sheet

The **full suite of 20 tests also passes with a real PostgreSQL 16 server as the database**. That test run caught a driver bug (SQLAlchemy 2.1 picking
psycopg v3) that would have crashed the backend on Postgres. GitHub Actions runs the tests plus a Docker build on every push.

**Deployment.** Backend: Render Web Service from `backend/Dockerfile` (free plan). Optional `DATABASE_URL` (e.g. a free Neon or

## 9. Limitations and future work

**Limitations**
- **Moderate accuracy.** Precision and recall are about 0.39 at the threshold, and about 3 in 10 demo thieves are scored Low. ENZERA
  prioritises inspections; it does not prove theft.
- **Learns "who was caught", not "how theft works".** Synthetic attacks on honest customers are not detected (6.5), and zero or
  reduced usage tends to lower the score. Label bias towards large, variable consumers is the likely cause.
- **Handcrafted features** still limit the tree model. The CNN ensemble shows more signal is available in the raw series (6.6).
- **Explanations are of the model, not of causes.** SHAP importance is correlational.
- **Short histories.** Uploaded files covering much less than three years drift strongly from the training data (PSI), so their
  scores are less reliable.
- **Isolation Forest** is only slightly better than random at ranking theft.
- **Free hosting.** Without `DATABASE_URL`, SQLite is reset whenever Render restarts. The backend sleeps after 15 minutes (30-60 s cold
  start). Logins expire on restart unless `SECRET_KEY` is set.
- **Demo security.** Demo accounts are public in the README, and passwords are SHA-256 hashed without per-user salts. That is fine
  for a demo, not for production.

**Future work**
- Deploy the **XGBoost + CNN ensemble** via ONNX/`onnxruntime`, with gradient-based explanations for the CNN part.
- Add **synthetic attacks to the training data** (as Jokar et al. do) and **self-comparison rules** (a sudden drop vs the customer's own
  history or neighbours).
- An **MLflow model registry** with automatic promotion, and **scheduled retraining** from `/labels`.
- Turn drift monitoring into **alerts**, and store drift history over time.
- **Proper user management** (salted password hashing such as bcrypt, OAuth/SSO) and a managed PostgreSQL by default.

## 10. Conclusion

ENZERA turns raw smart-meter data into a ranked, explained and calibrated inspection list, and supports the full inspection
workflow around it. On SGCC, XGBoost on 17 interpretable features, with gap-aware cleaning, class weights, a validation-tuned threshold
and Platt calibration, reaches ROC-AUC 0.798 and PR-AUC 0.376, and finds 43.5% of thieves in the top 10% of customers. Every model
change was adopted only under a decision rule fixed in advance. This let us keep a real improvement (gap-aware + weekly features,
+0.043 PR-AUC) and reject changes that did not hold up (SMOTE, tuning, the CNN alone, a retrained candidate). A CNN ensemble shows
where further accuracy lies, and a synthetic-attack test shows the most important limitation: a model trained on inspection labels
learns who was caught. The complete system is live and reproducible from the public repository. It includes TreeSHAP reasons,
waterfall and what-if; an inspection planner; cases with roles and an audit trail; retraining with a promotion gate; drift
monitoring; MLflow tracking; SQLite/PostgreSQL support; 20 tests and CI.
